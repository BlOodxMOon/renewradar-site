Many careful people start tracking subscriptions in a spreadsheet: one row per service, columns for price, cycle and next date. It is a good instinct, and a spreadsheet is better than nothing. But after a few months, most of these sheets go stale.

## What spreadsheets do well

- **Flexibility.** You can add any column you like, such as card used or shared with.
- **Free and familiar.** Everyone has a spreadsheet app.
- **Calculations.** With a few formulas you can total monthly and yearly costs.

## Where spreadsheets fall short

### They do not remind you

This is the big one. A spreadsheet only helps when you open it. Renewals happen whether you look or not. The whole point of tracking subscriptions is to act before the charge, and a sheet cannot tap you on the shoulder.

### Dates do not update themselves

After a monthly subscription renews, its next date in the sheet is wrong until you change it. Miss a few updates and you can no longer trust the sheet.

### Phones and spreadsheets do not mix well

Adding a trial while you sign up on your phone means opening a sheet on a small screen. Many people intend to add it later and never do.

- **Reminders** on your phone before every renewal and before every free trial ends, at the timing you choose.
- **Automatic roll-forward.** When a renewal date passes, the next one is calculated from the billing cycle.
- **Trials as a separate section**, with days left and the price after the trial.

It keeps some of the spreadsheet's flexibility too: a notes field for details like the card or account, a Personal, Work or Business label, and categories for spending reports.

## When a spreadsheet is still useful

- For detailed household budgets that include far more than subscriptions.
- For sharing a list with someone who does not use the app.
- For keeping a long-term archive.
